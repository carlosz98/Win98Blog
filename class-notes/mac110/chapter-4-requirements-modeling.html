Chapter 4: Requirements Modeling

Phase 2, Systems Analysis. The analysis phase has three activities: requirements modeling, data and process modeling, and development strategies. Its deliverable is the system requirements document. This chapter is the first activity: finding out what the new system must do.

Five categories of system requirements:

  1. Outputs: what the system produces (reports, screens, receipts).

  2. Inputs: data entering the system.

  3. Processes: logical rules applied to the data.

  4. Performance: speed, volume, capacity, availability, reliability.

  5. Controls: security, privacy and data integrity measures.

Also consider scalability (can it handle future growth in volume and data storage) and total cost of ownership (TCO), which includes hidden indirect costs such as user support and downtime.

Team-based approaches:

AgileWorking software in short iterationsPrototypes adjusted to users; simple tools like whiteboards and sticky notes; Scrum uses sprints, a product owner, a Scrum master and the team

Modeling tools:

  • Functional decomposition diagram (FDD): a top-down tree of business functions broken into processes. A CASE tool can generate DFDs from it.

  • UML use case diagram: actors (stick figures) and the use cases (ovals) they trigger.

  • UML sequence diagram: the order of messages between objects for one use case.

Fact-finding answers who, what, where, when, how and why, first for the current system, then asking whether each thing should be done that way.

The interview, seven steps:

  1. Determine the people to interview.

  2. Establish objectives for the interview.

  3. Develop questions: open-ended ("What do you think of the current system?"), closed-ended (yes/no or a specific fact) and range-of-response (rate it 1 to 10).

  4. Prepare for the interview.

  5. Conduct it, listening actively and avoiding leading questions.

  6. Document it right away.

  7. Evaluate it for bias or missing facts.

Other fact-finding techniques: document review; observation (watch out for the Hawthorne effect, where people work differently when watched); questionnaires and surveys (including fill-in forms); sampling (systematic, every nth record; stratified, a set number from each group; random); and research (journals, the web, site visits). Brainstorming can be structured (each person in turn) or unstructured.

Documentation: record facts as they are collected, using tools such as word processors, spreadsheets, databases, presentation software, and information management software.

Likely test points

  • The five requirement categories, with an example of each.

  • JAD vs. RAD: requirements model vs. finished system; the four RAD phases.

  • The seven interview steps and the three question types.

  • The three kinds of sampling and the Hawthorne effect.

  • Scalability and TCO: why they matter before design starts.
